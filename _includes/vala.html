Alcohol has been the main cause of an ongoing mortality crisis in Russia with the life expectancy of men being only 57.6 years in 1994 (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9259651/). It is estimated that at that time up to 33% of total deaths in the country were alcohol-related (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12410782/). In the early 2000s, when the average yearly consumption of pure ethanol had reached over 20 liters, the government started taking various measures to combat this problem (https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736(19)32265-2/fulltext). This issue is not new, as alcohol has had a deep cultural significance in Russia for centuries. Drinking has been central in social rituals, celebrations and the male identity. Over-indulging is quite normalized and this can be traced all the way back to the Tsarist era, in the 15th century, when alcohol sales were a major revenue source for the state. Since then the state has had inconsistent policies, for example Gorbachev's anti-alcohol campaign and deregulation following the collapse of the Soviet Union. These have contributed to swings in consumption and more importantly, mortality. A common Russian drinking pattern is a binge consumption of spirits, such as vodka (https://www.aiha.com/wp-content/uploads/2015/07/14-Cultural-Aspects-of-the-Social-Attitudes-Toward-Alcohol-in-Russia.pdf). The long-term impacts of this drinking culture are visible when comparing mortality rates across countries. As shown below, Russia consistently has one of the highest alcohol-related death rates globally, far exceeding those of Western nations like France and the United States. The figure highlights how deeply embedded and deadly Russia's drinking patterns have been over time (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results?params=gbd-api-2021-public/16c7bd3529fd9d806de2833f33d6c1b0).
