Introduction

Alcohol has been the main cause of an ongoing mortality crisis in Russia, with the life expectancy of men being only 57.6 years in 1994 (Shkolnikov et al., 1998). It is estimated that at that time, up to 33% of total deaths in the country were alcohol-related (Nemtsov, 2002). In the early 2000s, when the average yearly consumption of pure ethanol had reached over 20 liters, the government started taking various measures to combat this problem (Zaridze et al., 2014).

This issue is not new, as alcohol has had a deep cultural significance in Russia for centuries. Drinking has been central in social rituals, celebrations, and the male identity. Over-indulging is quite normalized and can be traced all the way back to the Tsarist era in the 15th century, when alcohol sales were a major revenue source for the state (Leon et al., 2009). Since then the state has had inconsistent policies, for example Gorbachev's anti-alcohol campaign and deregulation following the collapse of the Soviet Union, which contributed to swings in consumption and mortality. A common Russian drinking pattern is binge consumption of spirits, such as vodka, without food. This is especially common among men and contributes to alcohol dependency and poisoning (AIHA, 2015).

The long-term impacts of this drinking culture are visible when comparing mortality rates across countries. As shown in Figure 1, Russia consistently has one of the highest alcohol-related death rates globally, far exceeding those of Western nations like France and the United States. The few nations that come close to Russia's rates were also part of the former Soviet Union, like Belarus. The similarities in drinking culture are therefore expected. Despite some similarities to other former Soviet states, Russia's alcohol crisis stands out due to its scale and gender disparity. The spikes in mortality during the post-Soviet transition were driven by sudden changes in alcohol policy, economic instability, and the availability of non-beverage alcohols (Leon et al., 2009). The mortality impact was especially severe among working-age men, whose risk of dying from alcohol-related causes was several times higher than that of women (Shkolnikov et al., 1998).

Public health studies confirm that binge drinking of spirits played a central role in sudden deaths from poisoning, cardiovascular failure, and trauma (McKee et al., 2000; Zaridze et al., 2014). These patterns disproportionately affect men and contribute to a large gender gap in Years of Life Lost (YLLs) due to alcohol. Years of Life Lost is a statistical measure of premature mortality (WHO, 2021). Figure 2 shows how this burden manifests in Russia's YLL statistics, where men account for the overwhelming majority of premature mortality due to alcohol.
