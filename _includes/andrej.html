The Gender Gap

Russia's mortality crisis displays a striking gender disparity, with working-age men bearing most of the burden, the trend is often referred to as the 'male mortality crisis. Figure 7 illustrates the dramatic gap between male and female death rates among the working-age population, with male mortality peaking at approximately 20 deaths per 1,000 in the mid-1990s. Mortality began declining after 2005, possibly connected to the regulations introduced in 2006.
This gender disparity is further reflected in life expectancy figures. As shown in Figure 8, Russian men's life expectancy fell to 57.6 years in 1994, this put the life expectancy below the world average and it has not recovered to this day creating one of the world's largest gender gaps. On the other hand, Russian women lived about 10 years longer, comparable to life expectancy in developed nations. This create one of the world's largest life expectancy gaps between genders in the world.

In the introduction (Figure 1), we saw that Russia leads in alcohol-related deaths. According to official Russian statistics (Figure 9), alcohol-related deaths among working-age males accounted for around 4-5% of all deaths during the peak in the 1990s. This was approximately five times lower than the leading cause of death—diseases of the circulatory system—which accounted for about 23% of deaths in working age men. It's important to note that alcohol-related deaths here refer only to those directly attributed to alcohol consumption under the ICD-10 classification. This excludes many indirectly related fatalities, such as traffic accidents. As a result, the actual number is probably higher. A case study in the early 2000s estimated that hazardous drinking accounted for about 40% of all deaths in working age men a typical Russian city (Leon et al., 2007).

Figure 10 illustrates the correlation between alcohol consumption and mortality rates among working-age men in Russia from 1990 to 2021. The correlation is especially clear during three periods: a sharp rise after the Soviet collapse (1991-1994), a brief dip during the 1998-1999 economic crisis, and a steady decline following alcohol control policies after 2006 (Leon et al., 2009; Lancet, 2019). After the Soviet Union collapsed in 1991, multiple men lost their jobs as factories closed and state-run industries were shut down. Many experienced a loss of income and social status, driving increased hazardous drinking as a coping mechanism (Tomkins et al., 2007). Cheap, non-beverage alcohols were often consumed, leading to poisoning and early death (Leon et al., 2009). During this time, women were more likely to work in education, healthcare, and the service sector. These fields were less severely affected by the collapse. Cultural norms also discouraged heavy drinking behavior in women, this reinforced the large gender gap in alcohol-related mortality (AIHA, 2015). Seven years later, the economic crisis temporarily reduced alcohol consumption among both men and women, mostly because of decreased affordability. However, harmful drinking resumed as soon as incomes recovered in the early 2000s (Leon et al., 2009). Following the 2006 Alcohol Market Reforms by Russia's government that caused alcohol sales to decline, we see the same happen to consumption and mortality, evident from Figure 10 (TAdviser, n.d.).

Russia’s male mortality crisis was fueled by a culture of hazardous alcohol use, especially vodka binge drinking among working-age men, leading to poisoning, heart failure, and injury. While government reforms from the mid-2000s reduced vodka consumption and reduced deaths, persistent gender and regional gaps highlight ongoing challenges.
