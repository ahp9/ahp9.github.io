In the introduction (Figure 1), we saw that Russia leads in alcohol-related deaths. According to official Russian statistics, alcohol-related deaths among working-age males accounted for around 4-5% of all deaths during the peak in the 1990s. This was approximately five times lower than the leading cause of death—diseases of the circulatory system—which accounted for about 23%. It's important to note that alcohol-related deaths here refer only to those directly attributed to alcohol consumption under the ICD-10 classification. This excludes many indirectly related fatalities, such as traffic accidents. As a result, the actual number is higher; a case study in the early 2000s estimated that hazardous drinking contributed to 40% of all deaths in one Russian city (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17574092/).

Alcohol-related causes include: Chronic alcoholism, alcoholic psychosis, encephalopathy, dementia, Alcoholic liver disease and Unintentional poisoning by alcohol (ICD-10 codes: F10, K70, X45). Diseases of the circulatory system include: Rheumatic heart disease, Hypertensive disease, Ischaemic heart disease, Cerebrovascular disease (ICD-10 codes: I00-I99).

Figure x illustrates the correlation between alcohol consumption and mortality rates among working-age men in Russia from 1990 to 2021. The correlation is especially clear during three periods: a sharp rise after the Soviet collapse (1991-1994), a brief dip during the 1998-1999 economic crisis, and a steady decline following alcohol control policies after 2006 (Leon et al., 2009; Zaridze et al., 2014). After the Soviet Union collapsed in 1991, multiple men lost their jobs as factories closed and state-run industries were shut down. Many experienced a loss of income and social status, driving increased hazardous drinking as a coping mechanism (Tomkins et al., 2007). Cheap, non-beverage alcohols were often consumed, leading to poisoning and early death (Leon et al., 2009). During this time, women were more likely to work in education, healthcare, and the service sector. These fields were less severely affected by the collapse. Cultural norms also discouraged heavy drinking behavior in women, this reinforced the large gender gap in alcohol-related mortality (AIHA, 2015). Seven years later, the economic crisis temporarily reduced alcohol consumption among both men and women, mostly because of decreased affordability. However, harmful drinking resumed as soon as incomes recovered in the early 2000s. Following the 2006 Alcohol Market Reforms by Russia's government that caused alcohol sales to decline, we see the same happen to consumption and mortality, evident from Figure x.
