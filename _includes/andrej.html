In the beginning, figure 1, we have seen that Russia is the leading country in terms of alcohol-related deaths. Using the offical Russian statistics, we can see that the alcohol related deaths for working age males acounted for around 4-5% of all deaths during the peaks in 1990s. Compared to the leading cause of death which is circulatory diseases that was around 23%, this is a significant number. It is also important to note that the alcohol-related deaths are deaths directly caused by alcohol consumption by the ICD-10 classification. This means that there are many more deaths that are indirectly caused by alcohol consumption e.g. traffic accidents which are not included in this number. This means that the actual number of deaths is higher, and a case study in early 2000s estimated that hazaradous drinking in a city contributed to 40% of all deaths (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17574092/).

The plot illustrates a strong correlation between alcohol consumption and mortality rates among working-age men in Russia. From the early 1990s to the mid-2000s, both alcohol consumption (measured in liters per capita) and mortality rates (per 1,000 population) exhibit a similar trend, with peaks in the mid-1990s and a gradual decline after 2005. This parallel movement suggests that hazardous drinking significantly contributed to the high mortality rates during this period. The decline in both metrics after 2005 aligns with public health interventions and stricter alcohol regulations, highlighting the impact of reducing alcohol consumption on improving mortality outcomes.
